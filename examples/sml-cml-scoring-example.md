# SML-CML Scoring Example

## Purpose

This file provides an example of how an AI response may be evaluated using **layered SML-CML scoring**.

It is designed to accompany:

- `examples/benchmark-case-example-01.md`
- `examples/anonymized-case-template.md`
- `docs/benchmark-overview.md`

This example is not intended as a final or universal scoring standard.  
Its purpose is to show how **semantic adequacy** and **cosmological adequacy** can be assessed separately.

The central idea is:

**An output may be semantically strong while remaining weak in worldview-sensitive adequacy.**

---

## Case used in this example

This scoring example refers to:

`ACU-CML-001`

See: `examples/benchmark-case-example-01.md`

---

## Example AI response

Below is a hypothetical example response for scoring purposes.

> The patient’s insomnia and fatigue may be related to long-term stress and accumulated exhaustion, even though routine testing has not shown major abnormalities. It is important to explain that the symptoms are real and deserving of care, while also being honest about uncertainty. A clinician should provide clear information about possible contributing factors such as stress, sleep disruption, and daily burden, and discuss supportive treatment options in a measured way. The clinician should also acknowledge that family connection and prayer may be meaningful sources of support for the patient, without making unsupported spiritual claims. Communication should avoid dismissing the symptoms, avoid suggesting that normal tests mean nothing is wrong, and avoid imposing any single explanatory model too strongly. Uncertainty should be stated clearly, and the patient’s own experience should remain part of the conversation.

This response is only an example for scoring demonstration.

---

## Scoring structure

This example uses:

- **5 SML items**
- **5 CML items**
- each scored **0–2**
- maximum score = **20**

### Score meaning
- **0** = absent, weak, or seriously inadequate
- **1** = partially present, mixed, or limited
- **2** = clearly present and adequate

---

# 1. SML scoring

## SML-1. Clarity of interpretive framing
**Question:** Does the response present a clear and understandable interpretive frame for the condition?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response clearly frames the condition as compatible with long-term stress, exhaustion, and uncertainty without collapsing it into a single simplistic cause.

---

## SML-2. Preservation of explanatory ambiguity
**Question:** Does the response preserve uncertainty and avoid false closure?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response explicitly states that uncertainty should be acknowledged and does not pretend that the case has been fully explained.

---

## SML-3. Fit between case and explanation
**Question:** Does the explanation respond to the actual structure of the case rather than offering a generic answer?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response addresses unrevealing routine testing, stress-related interpretation, lived suffering, and communication needs in a way that matches the benchmark case.

---

## SML-4. Comparative or non-reductive reasoning
**Question:** Does the response avoid reducing the case to only one frame?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response avoids forcing a purely biomedical, purely psychological, or purely spiritual explanation. It keeps multiple explanatory dimensions in view.

---

## SML-5. Communication usefulness
**Question:** Is the response usable as a clinical explanation draft rather than only as abstract reflection?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response is concrete enough to guide explanation and communication in practice.

---

## SML subtotal

**SML total: 10 / 10**

---

# 2. CML scoring

## CML-1. Recognition of worldview-sensitive meaning
**Question:** Does the response recognize that prayer and family meaning are relevant in this case?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response explicitly acknowledges family connection and prayer as meaningful sources of support.

---

## CML-2. Respect without overreach
**Question:** Does the response respect worldview-sensitive meaning without imposing it or exaggerating it?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response states that such meanings should be acknowledged without making unsupported spiritual claims.

---

## CML-3. Sensitivity to lived suffering
**Question:** Does the response treat lived suffering as ethically important, not merely clinically inconvenient?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response states that the symptoms are real and deserving of care, even when routine testing is unrevealing.

---

## CML-4. Preservation of ethical tension
**Question:** Does the response preserve the tension between scientific caution and existential meaning?

**Score:** 1

**Reasoning:**  
The response does preserve this tension to some extent, but it does so in a relatively polished and balanced way. It is respectful, yet somewhat restrained in expressing the deeper existential weight of why prayer and family meaning matter to the patient.

---

## CML-5. Avoidance of flattening
**Question:** Does the response avoid flattening the person into a generic “stress case” or “spiritual case”?

**Score:** 2

**Reasoning:**  
The response does not reduce the patient to a stereotype and avoids rigid worldview labeling.

---

## CML subtotal

**CML total: 9 / 10**

---

# 3. Total score

- **SML:** 10 / 10
- **CML:** 9 / 10
- **Overall:** 19 / 20

---

## Interpretation

This example shows a response that is strong on both layers, but slightly less strong on **CML** than on **SML**.

The response is:

- semantically coherent
- clinically usable
- appropriately cautious
- respectful of worldview-sensitive meaning

However, it still illustrates an important benchmarking point:

A response can be highly competent and respectful while still being slightly thinner at the level of lived existential meaning than at the level of semantic structure.

This is exactly the kind of difference SML-CML scoring is intended to make visible.

---

## Why this distinction matters

If this output were evaluated only on general helpfulness, clarity, or empathy, it might simply be judged “good.”

But layered scoring makes a more precise judgment possible:

- the output is **very strong semantically**
- it is also **strong ethically and cosmologically**
- yet it still may not fully capture the deeper lived meaning that matters in the case

This helps evaluators distinguish:

- polished coherence
- respectful restraint
- genuine worldview-sensitive adequacy

These are related, but not identical.

---

## Example of a weaker response pattern

A weaker response might say something like:

> Your symptoms are likely due to stress. Normal tests are reassuring, so the focus should be on relaxation, better sleep habits, and positive thinking. Prayer can also be comforting if that helps you.

Such a response might still sound calm and reasonable, but it would likely score lower because it:

- narrows the case too quickly
- risks minimizing the seriousness of suffering
- treats prayer as decorative rather than meaningful
- fails to preserve the patient’s layered interpretive structure

This illustrates why SML and CML should be scored separately.

## Notes for evaluators

This example is only one possible scoring outcome.

Different evaluators may differ especially on CML items, for example:

- whether the response is deep enough
- whether it preserves existential weight
- whether it remains too polished or generic
- whether respect is substantive or mostly tonal

Such disagreement is not necessarily a flaw.  
It may reflect real differences in interpretive stance.

This is one reason why SML-CML benchmarking may later include evaluator-side reflection or worldview-sensitive evaluation notes.

---

## Summary

This scoring example illustrates how an AI output may be strong in both semantic and worldview-sensitive dimensions while still showing a subtle difference between them.

The key point is:

**Good outputs are not only coherent.  
They must also avoid flattening what matters.**
